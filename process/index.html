Process · How I work with AI

How I direct AI agents, and what review caught

This is the method behind the bakery build, written down after launch as a playbook: its ten rules, the loop every change runs, the incidents that turned into rules, and the first outside review of this site.

Ten principles fit on one page

  1. I own the what and the “done”. Claude owns the how, and shows proof.
  2. Proof means the command and its output, plus a screenshot for any screen change.
  3. The builder never grades its own work.
  4. Small steps, committed often, easy to undo.
  5. Staging first, always. I do the production push.
  6. Care in proportion to risk. Words and looks get a quick check; logic, money, data and permissions get the full gate.
  7. Lean beats thorough-looking. Every doc, test and agent costs something.
  8. One job per session, started from a short brief.
  9. Know the way back before going forward.
  10. I understand the load-bearing parts: money, data and access.

Every change runs the same loop

A change starts as a brief of one page or less and ends with my word on staging. The risk decides the middle: wording and layout get my staging check; logic, money, data and permissions get a scored review. The bakery store ran this loop, and my staging check caught a pricing bug that review had passed. This site skipped the staging step: it went live the night it was built.

Each incident became a rule

Overlapping test runs corrupted 16 settings, so runs take a lock; an agent resolved 13 review threads, so agents now answer threads but never resolve them. And because “Understood” was once taken as yes, a session now confirms its scope in one line before acting.

The bakery retro found the process worked and cost too much: 1.09 million words of docs, and ticket prompts of 3,000 to 7,000 words. The playbook now sets budgets, including three parallel lanes at most. This site’s build broke that one too, running four and then five lanes at once. Review round 1, below, is the correction.

Review round 1: what four reviewers caught

After the site went live, four reviewer agents read it the way a hiring loop would: a design lead, a web engineer, a copy editor and a panel reading as Epoch AI. I read their reports alongside them. Every fix is in the pull request, one small commit per change. These are the first nine findings, in the order they were ranked.

  1. The Epoch study’s premise was false. The study said Epoch’s chart cropped on a phone. That came from a headless Chrome window, which does not behave like a real phone screen. Three reviewers emulated a 390 px phone and the full axis rendered. The study was re-tested with device emulation at 390×844 and now describes what that test shows: on a phone, 2010 to 2026 is squeezed into the right fifth of the plot. The drawn “before” mock-up and its invented model label are gone.
  2. The home page led with commits per day. Four tiles and a chart measured how fast the agents worked, and one tile counted assumptions the bakery retro had disowned. The home page now opens on the product, the commit chart moved to this page as Figure 1, and that tile is gone.
  3. Scaffolding was live. A page template titled “«Page title»”, a prompt generator, an image manifest and comments naming build lanes were all public. They were deleted or moved out of the site. The two scripts the Epoch study links as source stay, on purpose.
  4. The site contradicted its own design-system pitch. About 1,400 lines of CSS sat inline across nine pages, with 102 hardcoded colors on the bakery page alone. Page styles moved into files built on the shared tokens, and color literals outside the token file went to zero.
  5. Citations pointed into a private repo. Dozens of file-and-line references opened nothing for a reader, and the live store was never linked. Unreadable citations were removed or replaced with excerpts, and the store is linked.
  6. The site was addressed to one employer. A “For Epoch” section on the About page told every other reader they were second choice, and it quoted the wrong job posting. The public pages are now neutral; the note to Epoch moved to an unlisted page that quotes the right posting.
  7. The bakery story had no outcome. It ended at “it shipped, 78 days late”. The outcome now explains the slip as a decision, names the measure that will decide success, and sets a date to report back.
  8. No page showed a decision I made. Each case study now has a section on one documented decision, the alternative I turned down, and why.
  9. This page undermined the method. It credited a build agent with the words on the page, published about 10,700 words of prompts, and contradicted itself twice: “Staging first, always” on a site that skipped staging, and a three-lane cap on a build that ran five. It was cut to the rules, the loop and this list, and the contradictions are now stated as what happened.

Who wrote what: agents drafted this site’s words and code. Four reviewer agents and I reviewed them in this round. My own rewrite in my own voice is still to come.

Every agent in these builds started from a written prompt. One of them, annotated, shows what a prompt has to carry.

Six words this page uses

  • Orchestrator. The agent session that splits a job into pieces, writes a prompt for each piece, and checks what comes back.
  • Lane. One of those pieces: one agent in its own terminal, given its own files so no two agents edit the same one.
  • Hook. A script that runs by itself whenever an agent edits a file, and blocks the edit if it breaks a rule, such as a hardcoded color.
  • Token. A named design value, like a color or a spacing step, defined once so every page uses the same one.
  • Parity check. A script that compares the tokens in code with the ones in Figma and lists any that differ.
  • Assertion. One automated yes-or-no check inside a test. The bakery suite runs 6,089 of them.
