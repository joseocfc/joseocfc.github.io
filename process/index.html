Process · How I work with AI

I own the what and the done. Agents own the how, and show proof.

This is the method behind the bakery build, written down after launch as a playbook. Below are its rules, the loop every change runs, excerpts from the real records, what the retro changed, and how this site was built the same way.

Ten principles fit on one page

  1. I own the what and the “done”. Claude owns the how, and shows proof.
  2. Proof, not summaries: the command and its output, and a screenshot for any screen change.
  3. The builder never grades its own work.
  4. Small steps, committed often, easy to undo.
  5. Staging first, always. I do the production push.
  6. Care in proportion to risk. Words and looks get a quick check; logic, money, data and permissions get the full gate.
  7. Lean beats thorough-looking. Every doc, test and agent costs something.
  8. One job per session, started from a short brief.
  9. Know the way back before going forward.
  10. I understand the load-bearing parts: money, data and access.

Every change runs the same loop

A change starts as a brief of one page or less and ends with my word on staging. The risk decides the middle: wording and layout get my staging check, while logic, money, data and permissions get a scored review.

The retro made the method cheaper

The bakery retro found that the process worked and cost too much: 1.09 million words of docs, nearly half the commits docs-only, and ticket prompts of 3,000 to 7,000 words. The playbook now sets budgets: a CLAUDE.md of 150 lines at most, a one-page brief and status, three parallel lanes, three review rounds.

Keep

  • A fresh reviewer, 9/10, capped roundsEvery failed round found a real bug.
  • Staging, then my phoneMy staging check caught a pricing bug that review had passed.
  • Record-and-ask; one decision at a time

Change

  • Ticket prompts of 3,000–7,000 wordsSize tickets to fit one session; keep briefs to one page.
  • Tests that pin markup and wordingTest behaviour; check looks by eye.
  • One test database shared by parallel sessionsEach lane gets its own data.

Drop

  • Essay commits
  • History in code comments
  • “Known reds”One “base red” was a real bug.

Each incident became a rule. Overlapping test runs corrupted 16 settings, so runs take a lock; an agent resolved 13 review threads, so agents now answer threads but never resolve them. And because “Understood” was once taken as yes, a session now confirms its scope in one line before acting.

This site was built in one night by one orchestrator and 15 worker terminals

The same method, smaller. On 7 November an orchestrator session opened four research lanes, read what they found, and wrote a 235-line spec that gives every file to exactly one lane. Then it opened five build lanes, each a terminal with one prompt (shell, charts, data, the Epoch study, and the words you’re reading), and after them the page lanes.

My playbook caps parallel lanes at three. This build ran four, then five at once, because no two lanes share a file, so no two workers can collide.

Models: the orchestrator ran on Claude Fable 5.1, and every worker from the build phase on ran on Claude Opus 5.5. Each lane’s prompt is published as written:

  • Researchthe Epoch dossier· the bakery case· the theme and Figma· other work
  • Buildshell· charts· data and images· Epoch study· copy
  • Pagesbakery case study· design system· this page· home and about
  • QA and FigmaQA audit· Figma file
