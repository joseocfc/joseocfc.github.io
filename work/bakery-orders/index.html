Case study · Community Food Co-op · June–November 2026

A bakery ordering system, designed and shipped by one designer directing AI agents

Community Food Co-op’s custom cake orders arrived in a shared inbox and were copied by hand onto carbon tickets. I replaced that with a WooCommerce plugin: a pickup-first cake builder, staff dashboards, printed tickets, emails and a Thanksgiving deli layer, live since 1 November 2026.

01 · The problem

Orders arrived as email and were copied onto carbon tickets by hand

The co-op sold custom cakes through its point-of-sale vendor’s storefront. Each order landed in a shared inbox and was re-keyed onto a three-color carbon ticket: white for the store, yellow for the deli, pink for the bakery. Cancellations were phone calls.

The deli’s Thanksgiving pre-orders were worse. The deli lead ran them alone: every order arrived as an email that couldn’t be searched, and each one was typed three times, into a season master, a pickup-day sheet and the store’s guide.

02 · Research

Decisions were made before the build, one question at a time

Before any feature, I had the agent put each open decision to me, one at a time, with a recommended answer. The first session, on 6 July, locked 12 product decisions and 8 build phases. Two shaped everything after: size is the only variation, with every other choice stored as structured data, and the browser “is fast but never trusted”, so the server rechecks the date and store at checkout.

Requirements argue from real examples. The deli’s 2025 workbook, 25 sheets with no formula linking one to another, was read cell by cell, and each finding became a rule:

One sentence kept the agents honest: “An undocumented assumption is a bug.” Every assumption the documents didn’t settle became a numbered row for me to sign off: 1,594 rows by launch.

03 · Decisions

Four decisions that changed what customers and staff do

The research produced 17 findings that changed the build. These four shaped what customers and staff see; a fifth, the Print button, gets the next section.

  1. A counter from day one, and no cap yet

    The bakery had no daily limit to give me, so the order cap launched switched off while a counter ran from day one, computed from real orders so no stored total can drift. A real cap can come later, from data.

  2. The cut-off is a single minute: 12:00 pm

    On 28 October I replaced the 72-hour lead time with two days and a noon cut-off. An order placed before noon counts from today; from noon on, it counts from tomorrow. A customer who opens the calendar at 11:55 and submits at 12:05 is refused by the server, by design.

  3. Pickup comes first

    Step 1 is the pickup: a store, then a calendar with three day states, and the step can’t advance without a date the server marks open. A customer never designs a cake for a day the bakery can’t make.

  4. One breakpoint, at 782px

    The storefront CSS has one media query, at 782px, and no tablet layout. A check fingerprints every byte outside that block, so the 375px layout I approved on my phone can’t move by accident.

04 · The Print button

I chose a Print button over auto-print, because the website can’t reach the printer

The plan had every ticket print itself the moment an order was paid, so nobody had to remember the step. Two facts stood in the way: the store runs on a web host that can’t see a printer on the co-op’s network, and the first version printed silently only because one Mac ran Chrome in kiosk mode. On 20 October I ruled it out: “i dont want tickets to automatically print when an order gets confirmed.”

Now a person decides. A Print click puts the job on a print list on the site; a small program on a computer beside the receipt printer collects it, prints the ticket and reports back. The browser’s own print dialog stays as a fallback, labeled “Print on this computer”.

The cost is the step the plan wanted to remove: someone has to click. The staff rules say so plainly: a ticket marked printed only means the print was sent, and the fix for a lost ticket is to print it again.

05 · The deli layer

The deli joined in October, and holiday ordering became a layer

From 6 to 25 October I ran a second discovery for the deli: a recorded interview with the deli lead, the workbook read above, requirements, a product spec and 18 tickets.

It shipped as a layer on the bakery plugin, not a second system. The Deli Dashboard is the deli lead’s own screen: the bakery’s list view asked for holiday orders only, with counts per day and store and a bulk “mark ready”. From it the deli prints, on letter paper, the season order list, the cooking totals, one sheet per order with its pickup slot, and the pickup list. Slots are numbered per store and day once that day’s ordering closes.

My requirement was that holiday ordering is “a layer on top of the plugin that can be turned on and off”. Each season is off, preparing or on, and only one can be on, so Christmas can be prepared while Thanksgiving is selling. Seasonal categories carry an on/off switch (is_season_open), and each product can be kept on sale past its season until a set date. Next year the deli switches the layer on; nothing is rebuilt.

The layer forced the biggest decision of the project. By mid-October the bakery storefront was on staging, the private test copy of the store, and could have launched alone. I chose one store for both counters, so the deli’s layer went into the same plugin, cart and checkout, and the store went live on 1 November, 78 days after the plan’s 15 August. The rest of the slip was August and September, spent on the Figma journey, the spec and the test suite.

06 · Design

The Figma journey was the spec, and every deviation was logged

I designed the flow at 375px in the co-op’s design-system file, from library tokens and components only, with each backend rule annotated on its frame. An overnight agent run on 5–6 August built 30 components, a 97-row copy map and 13 screens, with no hand-set colors, strokes, font sizes or radii.

On 6 August I approved the journey and sent back what the run had dropped from my wireframe, logged as 30 deviations. The copywriters’ 91 edits came back the same way, as a comparison that changed nothing until I approved.

In code, every screen was compared with its frame at 375px in 96 screenshots, until all 13 passed. The last gate was my own phone: “everything passed on my iPhone.”

07 · Build

WooCommerce does the plumbing; 64 modules do the bakery

The plugin doesn’t rebuild commerce. WooCommerce handles payment, cart and checkout; the plugin adds the builder, capacity, order statuses, tickets, emails and the staff screens. Every color and size comes from 200 named design tokens: “Never hand-author a brand value.”

08 · The AI workflow

A fresh reviewer and my phone decided when the agents’ work was done

Every commit is authored under my GitHub login, and 1,482 of 1,503 carry a co-author line for Claude, the AI that wrote the code. My part is on either side of it: the decisions and rules before, and the verdict after.

One agent session, the orchestrator, holds the plan and hands each ticket to a builder agent working in its own copy of the code. Builders can’t merge or publish to staging or the live store. On 25 October I handed the deli build to an orchestrator:

By the next morning all 18 deli tickets were merged and on staging.

The review rule is one line. A new reviewer each round scores the change out of 10; 9 passes and anything lower goes back, and the reviewer reruns the tests itself: “It does not trust the builder’s word.” The build’s own charts, from commits per day to review rounds, are on the Process page.

09 · Outcome

45 orders in the first week, from a store that couldn’t take them before

The store went live on the evening of 1 November. The first order came at 08:35 the next morning, Monday 2 November: an everyday bakery order. Holiday ordering opened on Wednesday 4 November and took six orders that day. By Sunday 8 November the system had taken 45 orders, 22 bakery and 23 holiday, for $4,091 in net sales.

The orders test the design decisions. 20 of the 23 holiday orders chose Wednesday 25 November for pickup, 13 at Downtown and 7 at Cordata: the pile-up the per-day, per-store sheets and their numbered slots were built for. Everyday bakery pickups spread across 15 days, from 6 to 26 November, at both stores, which is the “2 days / 12:00 pm” rule doing its job.

The plan assumed about 520 orders a year. The first week did 45, with Thanksgiving still two and a half weeks away, and 39 of the 45 came from customers new to the co-op’s webstore. Comparing with last November is apples to oranges, because bakery ordering didn’t exist online then; the honest figure is 45 orders that couldn’t have happened before.

10 · What I’d change

It worked, and it cost more process than it needed

I wrote a retro on 4 November and turned it into a playbook. Three things stay, three change, two go.

Keep

  • A fresh reviewer, a 9/10 pass mark and a cap on rounds.
  • Staging first, then my phone.My phone check caught a pricing bug the review had passed.
  • One decision at a time, with a recommendation.A bug found outside a ticket is written down and put to me, not fixed on sight.

Change

  • Ticket prompts of 3,000 to 7,000 words.The playbook now caps a brief at one page.
  • Tests that pin exact markup and wording.161 commits did nothing but update them.
  • One shared test database for parallel sessions.It needed a lock, and test orders still leaked into the mailing list.

Drop

  • Essay-length commit messages.The average subject ran to 243 characters.
  • Living with a failing test.One check failed on every run for weeks, for a data reason. Next time it gets fixed.
